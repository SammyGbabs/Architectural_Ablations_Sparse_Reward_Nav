\title{A construct-validity protocol for architecture comparisons in reinforcement learning}

\begin{document}

\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Architecture comparisons carry an implicit assumption: that the benchmark can actually
arbitrate the hypothesis, because the task contains the structure the architecture is meant
to exploit. When it does not, a
null is uninformative and a single-seed positive is unfalsifiable noise. Yet both are
routinely reported as evidence. We give a pre-registered protocol for deciding whether a
task can arbitrate an architecture hypothesis at all, before trusting any comparison run on
it: state the structural precondition, build a graded manipulation ladder targeting it, gate
each rung for learnability with one seed, promote only load-bearing rungs to multi-seed
evaluation, and attribute any induced hardness to the precondition rather than a confound
before comparing architectures. Instantiated on a claim from our own earlier, unpublished
work, that an inverted actor--critic asymmetry improves sparse-reward navigation, the
protocol first finds the single-seed advantage does not replicate (IQM $27.56$ vs $27.66$,
overlapping 95\% CIs, bootstrap $P(\text{inverted} > \text{symmetric}) = 0.25$), then shows \emph{why} the question cannot be settled on this
task at all, since no degradation of the observation reaches the required regime. Every rung
either ceilings, fractures into a give-up local optimum, or fragments across seed-selected
local optima. We report this as a worked negative outcome of the protocol, not as evidence
against asymmetry, and specify what a task that could arbitrate the hypothesis would need.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

The reproducibility literature has taught deep reinforcement learning to distrust a single
run. Seed variance can flip a conclusion, and much reported progress has not survived
multi-seed re-evaluation \citep{henderson2018matters,agarwal2021precipice}; the accepted
remedy is to report interquartile means (IQM) with stratified-bootstrap confidence intervals.
This closes one failure mode, mistaking noise for signal, but leaves a prior one untouched. A
comparison can be statistically impeccable and still meaningless, because if the benchmark
does not contain the structure the architecture is meant to exploit, multi-seed statistics
measure, with great precision, an effect the task was never capable of showing. The two cases
are observationally identical from inside the experiment. A null on a task where the effect is
genuinely absent, and a null on a task that could never have expressed it, produce the same
numbers. rliable can tell you a comparison is statistically sound; it cannot tell you it is
meaningful. Pre-registration can tell you that you did not fish for the result; it cannot tell
you the pond contains fish.

The gap has a common structure. An architecture hypothesis almost always asserts that
\emph{architectural property $A$ helps because it suits some structure $S$ of the problem}.
Inverted actor--critic asymmetry suits a policy that is hard to represent, convolution suits
spatial locality, attention suits long-range dependency. Such a hypothesis is only testable
on a task that actually contains $S$. Where $S$ is absent, $A$ has nothing to act on, so the
hypothesis predicts no effect \emph{by construction}, which makes a null uninformative and any
single-seed win noise dressed as evidence. Nothing in a standard train-and-evaluate loop
flags this. The task fails to pose the question, and the experiment answers a different one,
both without visible symptom.

We arrived at this through a concrete claim of our own. In earlier, unpublished experiments
of ours, an inverted actor--critic asymmetry, a policy network deeper than its value network,
appeared to beat a symmetric baseline at matched budget on a sparse-reward indoor-navigation
task, on a single seed. The claim comes apart on re-examination, but \emph{how} it comes
apart is the useful observation, because it asked a question the task could not answer.

This paper makes two contributions. The first is a reusable protocol
(\S\ref{sec:protocol}) for deciding whether a task can arbitrate a given architecture
hypothesis before trusting any comparison run on it. The second is a worked negative outcome,
in which no pre-registered degradation of the navigation task induces the regime the
hypothesis needs. Throughout, this is a statement about \emph{the task}, not the hypothesis.
We show this task class cannot arbitrate inverted asymmetry, and specify
(\S\ref{sec:need}) what a task that could would need, not that asymmetry fails in
reinforcement learning.

\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Reproducibility and evaluation.} Deep RL results are highly sensitive to seeds,
implementation details, and hyperparameters, and single-run comparisons frequently fail to
replicate \citep{henderson2018matters}; the standard response is distributional statistics
rather than point estimates \citep{agarwal2021precipice}. We adopt this machinery throughout.
Our concern is orthogonal, since these methods establish whether a measured difference is
\emph{statistically} real, not whether the task could have produced it at all.

\paragraph{Construct validity.} That benchmarks may not measure what they are taken to
measure is not new. Under the banner of \emph{construct validity}, originating in
psychological measurement theory \citep{cronbach1955construct} and recently imported into
machine-learning evaluation critique \citep{raji2021everything}, recent work develops explicit
validity conditions for predictive benchmarking \citep{freiesleben2025benchmarking} and
documents pervasive weaknesses across hundreds of benchmarks \citep{bean2025measuring}. That
literature is almost entirely \emph{diagnostic}, in that it reviews existing benchmarks and
names how their validity fails. What it does not provide is a constructive, executable
procedure a researcher can run \emph{before} trusting a particular comparison. Our
contribution is exactly that instrument, for one sharply-defined validity question, with a
pre-registered, pass/fail outcome.

\paragraph{Asymmetric actor--critic.} The assumption that actor and critic share topology and
capacity has been questioned from more than one direction. One line studies the
\emph{small-actor} regime, where shrinking the actor degrades performance through value
underestimation \citep{mastikhina2025optimistic}. A different sense of asymmetry gives the
critic privileged state while the actor sees only observations \citep{pinto2018asymmetric},
an asymmetry of \emph{information} rather than capacity. We examine capacity in the direction
opposite the small-actor line. That capacity asymmetry can produce pronounced effects on
tasks containing the relevant structure sharpens rather than undercuts our claim, since
whether a \emph{given} task can reveal it must itself be established.

\section{Results: from re-evaluation to task validity}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Phase 1: the reported effect does not replicate}
\label{sec:phase1}

The environment is a $20\times20$ residential grid (\texttt{Discrete(5)} actions, a 16-D
observation, reward $R(L)=30-0.2L$ for an $L$-step success), sparse in the sense that reward
arrives only on reaching the target room, a setting long recognised as hard because
uninformative early experience gives almost no gradient to follow
\citep{andrychowicz2017her}. Holding the original single-seed claim to a 10-seed standard
with IQM and 95\% stratified-bootstrap CIs \citep{agarwal2021precipice}, across four PPO
\citep{schulman2017ppo} capacity ratios at fixed budget, the advantage disappears. IQM
$27.56$ (inverted) versus $27.66$ (symmetric), overlapping CIs, and probability of
improvement $P(\text{inverted}>\text{symmetric})=0.25$. A companion claim of
${\approx}12.5\times$ PPO-over-DQN sample efficiency dissolves the same way once measured in
environment steps rather than episodes, because the two families' step-to-90\% distributions
overlap and the slowest configuration is a PPO one (Appendix~\ref{app:phase1}). The primary
finding is that the effect does not replicate, but a null here is not yet an answer. The 16-D
observation hands the agent its position and distance-to-target, so the optimal policy is
close to a smooth distance-descent that a shallow network represents as well as a deep one,
which leaves the precondition the hypothesis needs unmet \emph{by construction}, and a null is
exactly what the theory predicts.

\subsection{Phase 2: a pre-registered ladder shows the task cannot arbitrate the hypothesis}
\label{sec:phase2}

Can any degradation of the observation put the task into the regime the hypothesis needs, a
policy genuinely hard to represent while the value stays smooth, without simply making the
task unlearnable? We answer with a pre-registered experiment. Design, hypotheses,
falsification criteria, and per-outcome interpretations were frozen before any run, and a
dated amendment trail records every subsequent decision. The independent variable is a ladder
of observation wrappers over the frozen environment, monotone in policy-relevant information
removed; the primary endpoint is the inverted$-$symmetric IQM gap. Because a floor effect
would masquerade as the target regime, each rung is first single-seed-gated for learnability
before any load-bearing cell is promoted to 10 seeds. The ladder tests four degradation
mechanisms: pure feature removal (dropping global position and distance-to-target),
flicker combined with frame-stacking, targeted aliasing (dropping the region one-hot), and
proximity noise.

The ladder was pre-registered before any run and extended by four dated amendments as each
rung proved insufficient. The initial pure-removal rungs ceilinged, so flicker with
frame-stacking was added; that in turn either ceilinged ($p=0.7$) or fractured ($p=0.8$), so
targeted aliasing was added, following an analysis of what our own data showed about the
task's difficulty structure; proximity noise was added as the final mechanism. Each amendment
was committed before its runs, and the interpretation of each possible outcome was fixed in
advance. The pre-registration document and its full amendment trail are included in the
supplementary material (Appendix~\ref{app:repro}).

Guided by the POMDP-difficulty literature, in which static feature removal is often
insufficient and temporal hidden state is what makes policies hard
\citep{hausknecht2015drqn,morad2023popgym}, the mechanisms are, in detail:
\emph{pure removal} (drop global position, then distance-to-target);
\emph{flicker $+$ frame-stack}, masking each frame with probability $p$
and stacking the last $k=4$ \citep{mnih2015human}, so any hardness remains representable by a
feedforward policy rather than requiring recurrence \citep{kapturowski2019r2d2};
\emph{targeted aliasing} (drop the region one-hot, so room, hallway, and doorway cells are
indistinguishable from a single frame); and \emph{proximity noise} (flip each proximity bit
with probability $q=0.3$).

Table~\ref{tab:ladder} reports every claim-grade cell. Three readings are decisive.
\textbf{First, the ceilings are real ceilings}, not slow learners or wall-avoidance floors.
The 200k ceiling cells reach 100\% success on all three rooms with tight CIs, proximity noise
does so at a collision rate of $0.00$, and even the hardest \emph{learnable} flicker
($p=0.7$) reaches the ceiling given budget, with 9/10 seeds at full success (the tenth
plateaus partway, which is why that cell's bathroom SR reads 0.93; Appendix~\ref{app:phase2}
gives the per-cell detail and the optimal-path ceiling reference).

\textbf{Second, the one cell that leaves the ceiling does not become hard-but-learnable; it
fragments, and the two budgets together are the finding.} At 200k, $p=0.8$ looked like a clean
systematic fracture. IQM collapsed to $8.95$, the kitchen was abandoned in 10/10 seeds and the
bedroom solved in 10/10, failures were timeouts rather than collisions, and only three
distinct per-room outcome patterns appeared, suggesting a single give-up local optimum shared
across runs. That cell, however, ran at 200k, while the $p=0.7$ cell had required 500k.

To eliminate this budget asymmetry, we re-ran the $p=0.8$ cell at $10$ seeds $\times$ 500k,
matching the $p=0.7$ budget. The aggregate barely moves, $13.24$ against $8.95$
with substantially overlapping intervals, so mean return is not statistically distinguishable
between budgets. The \emph{distribution}, however, changes completely, with six distinct
per-room patterns now appearing (Table~\ref{tab:perseed}). The kitchen, abandoned by every
seed at 200k, is solved by five of ten;
conversely two seeds abandon the \emph{bedroom}, the one room every seed solved at 200k. Only
two of ten seeds solve all three rooms. The 200k reading, ``systematic near/far give-up'',
does not survive the matched budget. What both budgets together support is stronger. At
$p=0.8$ the task sits in a regime of \emph{landscape fragmentation}, where multiple
locally-optimal \emph{reactive} policies partition the outcome space by room and seed
selection determines which one a run finds. Read down the ladder this is a graded trend, not
an isolated cell, since distinct per-seed patterns rise monotonically with degradation
severity, $1/1/1/2/3/6$ across the six cells. Either way the rung is disqualified for an
architecture comparison, and for the same reason, because a gap measured on it would reflect
which architecture more often lands in which basin, not whether $A$ suits $S$.

\textbf{Third, this exhausts the axis.} Across removal, flicker, aliasing, and sensor noise,
the pre-registered set of single-map observation degradations, no rung meets the precondition.
The pre-registered primary endpoint is defined only on a rung passing the attribution guard of
step~5 below, and no rung did. The gap was therefore never computed on a qualifying cell, and
Table~\ref{tab:ladder} is symmetric-only for exactly that reason, since the non-computation
\emph{is} the result. Every aggregate reported here regenerates from the released
per-seed CSVs under a seeded bootstrap (Appendix~\ref{app:repro}).

\section{Why the task resists the ladder}
\label{sec:why}

Three properties of the task together explain why no manipulation induced a hard-to-represent
policy. First, the observation is \emph{redundant}, because multiple channels each
independently suffice for competent navigation, so removing any one leaves the others to carry
the load. Six of sixteen dimensions can be removed with all three rooms still solved in 100\%
of episodes (Appendix~\ref{app:mechanism}). Second, the optimal policy is \emph{reactively
solvable}, a simple function of the current observation, so manipulations that attack the
availability of information, such as flickering, do not change the complexity of the function
mapping observations to actions. Third, a single fixed layout is \emph{memorisable}, since a
policy can encode routes specific to this map rather than compute them from the observation,
which leaves it robust to noise in any one channel. The precondition the hypothesis requires,
a policy hard to represent while the value function stays smooth, is a property of the task's
\emph{decision structure}, not of its observation vector. Degrading the observation can make
information scarcer, narrower, or noisier without making the underlying decision function any
harder to represent.

\section{The protocol}
\label{sec:protocol}

The navigation study instantiates a problem that recurs whenever architectures are compared
empirically. We distil it into a reusable procedure, stated in task- and hypothesis-agnostic
terms.

\begin{center}
\fbox{\parbox{0.955\linewidth}{
\textbf{Can this task arbitrate this architecture hypothesis?} \emph{Given ``property $A$ helps
because it suits problem structure $S$'':}
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{State the precondition.} Write down $S$ explicitly, and the observable signature
  of $S$ present versus absent. If you cannot name $S$, you cannot evaluate the claim.
\item \textbf{Design a manipulation ladder.} Build graded task variants that monotonically
  vary the presence of $S$, holding reward, dynamics, budget, and optimiser fixed.
\item \textbf{Gate each rung for learnability, one seed, cheap.} Confirm a \emph{standard}
  agent can still learn each rung before spending statistical compute. Discard rungs where the
  baseline floors, since an unlearnable rung looks ``hard'' but tests nothing.
\item \textbf{Promote load-bearing rungs to multi-seed.} For rungs a conclusion rests on, run
  enough seeds for stratified-bootstrap CIs; report IQM $+$ CI, never single-seed points.
\item \textbf{Read the ladder, and attribute the hardness.} If a rung is
  \emph{hard-but-learnable}, do not stop at ``it's hard.'' Verify the hardness is
  attributable to $S$ specifically, not to a confound such as an optimisation pathology
  or raw sensory deprivation. A rung can be hard the \emph{wrong} way, in which case a gap
  measured there reflects which architecture escapes the pathology more often, not whether $A$
  suits $S$. Only an $S$-attributable rung qualifies. If none does, the task cannot arbitrate
  the hypothesis; report that, with the mechanism.
\end{enumerate}
}}
\end{center}

Two design choices are part of the contribution, not lab habit. \textbf{Pre-registration} of
the ladder and, critically, of a per-outcome \emph{interpretation table} fixed before any run,
prevents the search from degenerating into architecture-fishing, because with readings
committed in advance, ``we tried variants until one favoured our architecture'' is
structurally impossible, and every escalation is a dated amendment with a frozen meaning.
Pre-registration is long established in medicine and psychology
\citep{chambers2013registered} and has seen dedicated efforts in machine learning
\citep{preregistration2020,preregistration2021}, whose motivation, that rewarding only
positive results suppresses informative negative findings, is closely aligned with ours.
\textbf{The gate/multi-seed split} makes exhaustive laddering affordable, since a single-seed
gate is one training run, so many candidate rungs are screened for the price of one
claim-grade cell. Together they turn ``our architecture idea didn't win'' into a falsifiable,
cheap diagnostic about the benchmark. Our own $p=0.8$ rung is the cautionary case, being
hard-but-not-ceiling yet hard the \emph{wrong} way, so it is disqualified by step~5
rather than by its difficulty.

\subsection{A positive control: the protocol returns \emph{yes} where the precondition holds}
\label{sec:poscontrol}

A procedure that has returned only its negative branch invites a fair objection. Is it a
detector, or a rejection stamp? We run it where the precondition can be inserted and removed
at will, recovering a \emph{known-true} effect, with $A=$ convolution and $S=$ spatial
locality. The ladder applies a fixed permutation to a fraction $q$
of MNIST pixel positions. Every rung is a \emph{bijection}, a fixed relabeling of input units,
so information is provably preserved and only locality is destroyed as $q\to1$. We compare a
small CNN against a parameter-matched MLP (105.9k parameters each), 10 seeds, at a
sub-asymptotic budget. Predictions and interpretation table were pre-registered.

The CNN$-$MLP gap shrank monotonically from $+2.15$ points at $q=0$, with disjoint 95\% CIs,
to $-2.52$ at $q=1$ (full dose-response in Appendix~\ref{app:poscontrol}). The protocol
therefore reports that the task \emph{can} express $A$-suits-$S$ at $q=0$ and \emph{cannot} at
$q=1$, tracking the effect as it appears and vanishes. One pre-registered prediction missed,
and it missed informatively. We had frozen ``gap $\approx0$ at $q=1$'';
the observed $-2.52$, with disjoint CIs, says the convolutional prior is not merely
uninformative on non-local inputs but mildly \emph{harmful}, since the CNN is constrained to
seek local structure the scrambled task no longer contains while the position-agnostic MLP is
unencumbered. This does not weaken the control, and the effect still appears and vanishes with
$S$. But a prediction missed, and we let it stand rather than round it to the number we
pre-registered.

A researcher who evaluated only $q=1$ would not see a tie. They would see the
CNN lose by two and a half points and conclude that convolution is harmful for image
classification, a confident wrong answer from a sound multi-seed measurement of a meaningless
comparison. That is the structural position of anyone concluding ``asymmetry hurts in RL''
from our navigation null. The difference is only that here we can \emph{see} the error,
because the same protocol run one rung over recovers the effect in full.

\section{Designing a task that can arbitrate the hypothesis}
\label{sec:need}

The null is generative: the analysis that shows this task cannot arbitrate the hypothesis also
specifies what a task that could would look like. We state it as a property list a future
benchmark can be built against, rather than a vague call for harder tasks.
\textbf{(i)~Genuine, unroutable partial observability.} Hidden state that cannot be recovered
from any single observation \emph{and} cannot be memorised away, for example procedurally
generated layouts that vary across episodes, or a goal location revealed once and then
occluded, so no fixed-map shortcut exists and the optimal policy must integrate history every
episode. Procedurally generated suites already supply this in controlled form
\citep{cobbe2020procgen,chevalier2023minigrid}.
\textbf{(ii)~Non-redundant observation.} Each policy-relevant distinction encoded once, so a
targeted removal actually removes it rather than leaving parallel channels to carry the load,
for example an egocentric sensor with no global position channel, so that deleting the sensor
deletes the information rather than displacing the agent onto a redundant one.
\textbf{(iii)~A longer horizon with real credit assignment.} Enough temporal depth that the
optimal policy must compose sub-decisions, making its representation a compositional problem
rather than a one-step lookup, for example a task requiring a key to be fetched before a door
becomes passable, where the reward for the first sub-decision arrives only after the second.
\textbf{(iv)~A policy that is a sharp function even under full information.} Hardness intrinsic
to the optimal \emph{mapping}, with high-frequency structure and many decision boundaries,
rather than induced by hiding information, for example narrow corridors or hazard margins
where the correct action changes between adjacent states while the value function varies
smoothly across them, so that policy capacity is taxed while value stays comparatively smooth.
That last property is precisely the asymmetry the hypothesis is about, and precisely what the
present task lacks. A task meeting this specification would place the precondition inside a
learnable regime, and the comparison could be run where it is meaningful. Whether the
inverted-asymmetry advantage then appears is an open question this paper does not prejudge.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

The transferable lesson is a discipline, not a result. Before claiming a task confirms
or refutes an architecture hypothesis, establish whether it is able to falsify the claim. A
cheap, pre-registered manipulation ladder with single-seed learnability gates makes the check
affordable, and its outcome tells you where, or whether, to spend a claim-grade comparison. In
the terms of \S\ref{sec:related}, this operationalises the construct-validity question as a
prospective, pass/fail experiment run \emph{before} a comparison rather than a retrospective
critique delivered after it. Our negative result is deliberately narrow. We have shown that
this task, and by the mechanism of Appendix~\ref{app:mechanism} static single-map gridworld
navigation with a redundant, low-dimensional observation, cannot arbitrate the
inverted-asymmetry hypothesis. We have \emph{not} shown that inverted asymmetry fails in
reinforcement learning generally, and it may well hold on a task of the kind specified above.
A single-seed positive on such a task, our own starting point, is exactly what the protocol is
designed to catch.
